Are Electronic Shelf Label Labor Savings Overstated — and Is Dynamic Pricing the Real Driver? What the Data Shows

October 28, 2026·11 min read·AiESL Research
Critics are half right. Electronic shelf label labor savings are real but measured almost entirely by vendors, so published hour counts are best read as upper bounds; the strongest independent data shows the bigger financial effect comes from pricing — smaller, more frequent and mostly downward markdowns on perishables that raised gross margins — not from surge pricing, which a 180-million-observation study did not find. In short: “dynamic pricing” is a real driver of ESL value, but it is the markdown kind, not the price-gouging kind.

The question usually arrives in a pointed form: retailers justify electronic shelf labels with labor savings and accuracy, but critics say the real motive is the ability to change prices whenever they like. This page sets the two claims against what deployment data, peer-reviewed research and official sources actually show — and is explicit about where the evidence runs out. We build ESL systems, so we have a stake in the answer; every number below links to its source.

What Exactly Are the Two Claims?

Both sides agree ESLs make price changes cheap; they disagree about what retailers do with that.

 Industry claimCritics’ claim
Main benefitLabor saved on re-tagging; fewer pricing errorsThe ability to change prices at will
Effect on shoppersAccurate prices, faster promotionsSurge and surveillance pricing; fewer staff
Typical sourceVendor case studies, commissioned ROI studiesUnion and advocacy reports, legislators
Shared weaknessBoth lean on the same vendor marketing figures for labor savings.

What Does the Evidence Say About Labor Savings?

The direction is clear and the size is not. Every source agrees that changing a price centrally takes less staff time than printing and placing a tag. How many hours that saves per store has not been independently measured.

SourceWhat it saysIndependence
Walmart (company statement)A price change that took associates two days of walking the aisles now takes minutes.Retailer statement; no hour totals published.
Forrester TEI (2022)Composite 500-store grocer: 18-month payback, 277% five-year ROI, from price-change automation, faster in-store order picking and replenishment.Commissioned by Pricer; model built from three retailer interviews.
Vendor and reseller blogsClaims of 35–60 tag-change hours per supermarket per week, or 60–90% reductions.Usually unsourced.
AFL-CIO Tech Institute (Sept 2026)44,223–191,633 US grocery jobs and USD 1.6–6.9B of annual wages at risk.Advocacy estimate built on Pricer and VusionGroup marketing claims.

Sources: Walmart corporate news; Pricer / Forrester Consulting; AFL-CIO Tech Institute via Supermarket News. Links under Sources.

The irony is that the union estimate and the vendor brochures stand on the same foundation. If vendor hour claims are inflated, the job-loss estimate shrinks with them. A useful sanity check is the formula in our ROI guide: hours saved equal weekly price changes multiplied by the minutes each manual change takes. Most of the spread in published figures comes from those two inputs, not from the technology.

What Does the Evidence Say About Pricing?

Here the independent evidence is stronger — and it points to markdowns, not surges.

StudyDataFinding
Stamatopoulos et al. (2021)Management Science. International grocery retailer that adopted ESLs in 2015Gross margins rose substantially, beyond labor savings, with more units sold at lower prices — mainly in short-shelf-life categories. Price changes became smaller, more frequent and mostly decreases.
Chehrazi et al. (2025)Marketing Science. Pilots at a UK grocer with ESLs and an EU grocer adding ESLs plus expanded barcodesPrice-update frequency rose 54% and 853% respectively once inventory information frictions were addressed — labels alone do not make prices move; data about stock does.
Sanders et al. (2025)Working paper. 180M+ product-level observations, 114 stores in four US states, 2019–2024Essentially no change in short-lived price hikes after ESL installation; discounts became slightly more common.

Sources: INFORMS journals; UC San Diego Rady School of Management. The 2025 working paper had not been peer-reviewed at the time of writing.

Put together, the research describes a specific kind of “dynamic pricing”: grocers using cheap price changes to mark down perishables sooner and in smaller steps, selling more before products expire. That raises margin and plausibly reduces waste. It is the opposite of what “surge pricing” implies for shoppers. How an AI engine automates that markdown loop is explained in AI dynamic pricing with ESL.

Which “Dynamic Pricing” Are We Talking About?

Much of the debate is three different practices sharing one name.

PracticeWhat it meansEvidence with ESLsRegulatory status (US, 2026)
Markdown / clearance pricingLowering prices as stock ages or demand dropsDocumented: smaller, more frequent, mostly downward changesLong-standing, lawful practice
Surge pricingRaising prices temporarily at peak demandNot found in the largest US grocery dataset studiedUnder scrutiny; restricted in some proposals
Surveillance / personalised pricingDifferent prices for different shoppers based on their dataNot possible with a shelf label alone — every shopper sees the same tagBanned in New Jersey from 2026–27; restricted in other states

Sources: studies above; New Jersey Fair Price Protection Act (S3612/A4085) as reported by Grocery Dive. Not legal advice.

So What Is the Real Driver?

It depends on the store, and that is a testable claim, not a matter of opinion. In high-wage markets with heavy promotional calendars, labor on price changes is a large, direct saving. In fresh-heavy grocery, the margin effect of faster markdowns can match or exceed it, according to the only peer-reviewed measurement. In neither case does the evidence show surge pricing. Where critics have a fair point is that the industry’s labor figures are rarely audited — and that the same tool that makes markdowns cheap could make other price changes cheap too, which is why regulators are now drawing lines around personalised pricing.

For a chain deciding whether the investment stands up on its own numbers, our answer for mid-size grocery chains works through costs, phasing and the 2026 regulatory picture; the accuracy side of the case is covered in what 30 years of official data show.

How Can a Retailer Measure Both Effects in a Pilot?

Measure before you buy at scale, against control stores, for at least one full promotional cycle.

Frequently asked

Partly. The labor saving is real — Walmart says a price change that took two days of walking the aisles now takes minutes — but nearly every figure for how many hours are saved comes from vendors or vendor-commissioned studies. No independent before-and-after measurement of store labor hours has been published. Treat vendor hour estimates as upper bounds and measure your own in a pilot.

The strongest independent evidence suggests pricing matters at least as much as labor, but not in the way critics fear. A peer-reviewed study of a grocer that adopted ESLs found gross margins rose substantially, driven by smaller, more frequent and mostly downward price changes on short-shelf-life products. That is markdown agility, not surge pricing.

Current evidence says no. A working paper by Sanders, Stamatopoulos and Bray analysed more than 180 million product-level observations from 114 stores of a US grocery chain between 2019 and 2024 and found essentially no change in short-lived price hikes after the labels were installed; discounts became slightly more common.

Published figures range from a few hours to more than 50 hours per store per week, and almost all come from vendors. A Forrester study commissioned by Pricer modelled an 18-month payback for a composite 500-store grocer from price-change automation, faster in-store picking and replenishment. The real figure depends on how many price changes a store makes each week and how long each manual change takes — measure both in your own stores.

Because the labor saving vendors advertise is, from a worker’s perspective, lost hours. An AFL-CIO Tech Institute brief in September 2026 estimated 44,000 to 192,000 US grocery jobs at risk and USD 1.6–6.9 billion in annual wages, based on manufacturers’ own marketing claims, and urged policymakers to restrict the technology. Its figures inherit the same uncertainty as the vendor claims they rely on.

Run a pilot with matched control stores. Before and after installation, record time spent on price changes, the number of price changes per week, shelf-to-till price errors, gross margin and waste in fresh categories, and discount frequency. Compare treated and control stores over at least one full promotional cycle.
